A provider who is not enrolled with a payer cannot bill that payer cleanly. Claims can be denied or
held, and revenue is delayed until enrollment is approved. That is why credentialing deserves a plan
before a new provider's first day, not after the first denial.

This guide explains what credentialing and payer enrollment actually are, what you need to gather,
the steps from application to approval, why files stall, and how to keep enrollment from lapsing.

## What is provider credentialing?

Credentialing is the process of verifying a provider's qualifications: license, education, training,
board certification, work history, malpractice coverage, and any sanctions or disciplinary actions.
Payers do it to confirm a provider is qualified before allowing them in a network.

The word is often used loosely for three related things. They are not the same:

| Term | What it means | Who it involves |
|---|---|---|
| Credentialing | Verifying a provider's qualifications | The payer or credentialing body |
| Payer enrollment | Getting approved to bill and be paid by a specific payer, with an effective date | The provider, practice, and payer |
| Privileging | Permission to perform specific services at a specific facility | The hospital or facility |

Most practices need the first two. Privileging only applies if a provider works in a hospital or
surgery center.

## What do you need before you start?

Delays often begin with missing or inconsistent documents. Gather these first:

- **NPI** (National Provider Identifier) from NPPES
- **State medical license**, plus DEA and state controlled-substance registrations if applicable
- **Board certification** and education and training details
- **Work history**, with an explanation for any gaps
- **Malpractice insurance** certificate and claims history
- **CV**, ID, and a signed W-9
- **Practice details**: group NPI, tax ID, service locations, and billing address
- **Hospital affiliations**, if any

Store them in one organized place with expiration dates, since most of them expire.

## What are the steps from application to approval?

### 1. Gather and verify the documents

Check each document against payer requirements. Confirm that names, addresses, and tax IDs match
exactly across all records. Small mismatches are a leading cause of delay.

### 2. Build and attest the CAQH profile

Many commercial payers pull provider data from CAQH ProView, an online profile that providers keep
current. Complete every section, upload the documents, and authorize the payers you are applying to
so they can view it. CAQH profiles must be re-attested on a regular schedule, commonly every 120
days, so put the reminder on a calendar.

### 3. Submit the right applications

Each payer type has its own path:

- **Medicare:** enrollment through PECOS or the CMS-855 paper forms
- **Medicaid:** your state's enrollment portal, with rules that vary by state
- **Commercial plans:** each payer's own application, often combined with CAQH

### 4. Track every application and follow up

Payers rarely chase you. Set a follow-up schedule for each file, record who you spoke to, and
respond to requests for information the same day they arrive.

### 5. Confirm the contract and the effective date

For commercial plans, credentialing and contracting are separate steps. Approval of one does not
always mean the other is done. Get the effective date and provider ID in writing.

### 6. Load the provider into your systems

Only after the effective date is confirmed should billing staff schedule that payer's patients under
the provider and load the provider ID into your billing system.

### 7. Maintain enrollment

Credentialing is not a one-time event. Plan for:

- **Recredentialing**, which commercial payers commonly require every few years
- **Medicare revalidation**, which happens on a recurring cycle
- **Expiring documents**: license, DEA, malpractice, and board certification
- **Change reporting**: new locations, address changes, and ownership changes generally have
  deadlines for reporting to the payer

A lapsed enrollment can mean claims denied for dates of service you thought were covered.

## How long does credentialing take?

It varies widely by payer, by state, and by how complete the application is. For planning, assume
it takes months, not days, and that no one can promise a fixed date. The practical rules are:

- **Start early**, before the provider's start date.
- **Do not schedule a payer's patients** under a new provider until you have a confirmed effective
  date for that payer. Rules on retroactive effective dates differ by payer.
- **Prioritize payers** by how many of your patients they cover, and file those first.

## Why do credentialing files stall?

The same problems appear again and again:

- Incomplete applications or missing signatures
- Expired documents sent with the application
- Information that does not match across NPPES, CAQH, and the application
- Unexplained gaps in work history
- Payer requests that sit unanswered in an inbox
- Closed panels, where a payer is not accepting new providers in your area

Almost all of these are preventable with a checklist and a follow-up calendar.

## What should a credentialing tracker include?

A simple spreadsheet works if someone owns it. For each provider and payer, track:

| Field | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Payer and application type | Know what you filed and where |
| Submission date | Starts the follow-up clock |
| Payer contact and reference number | Saves time on every follow-up call |
| Status and next follow-up date | Prevents files from going quiet |
| Effective date and provider ID | Needed before billing |
| Recredentialing or revalidation due date | Prevents lapses |
| Document expiration dates | Catches the license or malpractice renewal in time |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between credentialing and enrollment?

Credentialing verifies a provider's qualifications. Enrollment is the approval to bill and be paid
by a specific payer. A provider needs both before claims can be paid for that payer's members.

### Can a provider see patients before credentialing is complete?

It depends on the payer and the situation. Seeing a payer's patients before the effective date can
leave you unable to bill for those visits. Confirm the rules for each payer before scheduling.

### Do I have to share my CAQH login with someone helping me?

Not necessarily. Many payers and CAQH allow delegated access, so the provider keeps ownership of the
account. Ask any vendor how they handle access before sharing credentials.

### How often do I need to update my CAQH profile?

CAQH requires regular re-attestation, commonly every 120 days, and any change to your information
should be updated promptly. Missing a re-attestation can stall applications and renewals.
