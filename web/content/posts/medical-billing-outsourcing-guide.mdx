Most practices do not decide to outsource medical billing on a whim. The decision usually follows a
pattern: claims sit unworked, denials pile up, a key biller leaves, or the owner realizes the front
office is spending more time on revenue cycle tasks than on patients. This guide explains when
outsourcing makes sense, what a billing partner should actually do, and what to ask before you sign
anything.

## When does outsourcing medical billing make sense?

Outsourcing is not right for every practice. It tends to fit when one or more of these is true:

- **Coverage is thin.** One biller carries the whole revenue cycle, and a sick day or resignation
  stops claims from going out.
- **Denials are growing.** Denied claims are being worked late or not at all, and nobody owns
  the rework.
- **Accounts receivable is aging.** Balances are sliding past 60 and 90 days because follow-up
  competes with other front-office work.
- **Growth is outpacing the team.** New providers, a second location, or a new service line
  added volume faster than you could hire.
- **Hiring is the bottleneck.** Finding, training, and keeping an experienced biller is taking
  longer than the problem can wait.

It is usually a weaker fit when your current team is performing well, your numbers are healthy, and
the real issue is upstream (for example, incomplete documentation from providers). A billing partner
cannot fix a documentation problem on its own, though it can help surface it.

## What does an outsourced billing partner actually handle?

A good scope is specific. "We do your billing" is not a scope. Here is what the work typically
includes, and where each piece lives:

| Task | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| Charge entry | Turning encounters into accurate, complete charges |
| Claim preparation and scrubbing | Checking claims for errors before submission |
| Claim submission | Sending claims to payers and tracking acceptance |
| Payment posting | Posting ERAs and EOBs and reconciling against expected payment |
| Denial management | Working, correcting, and resubmitting denied claims |
| Eligibility and benefits checks | Verifying coverage to prevent front-end denials |
| Patient statements | Billing patient balances and following up |
| A/R follow-up | Working aged claims with payers until resolved |

Two neighboring services are often bundled or sold separately: [medical coding](/services/medical-coding)
(assigning accurate codes from the chart) and [provider credentialing](/services/provider-credentialing)
(getting providers enrolled with payers so claims can be paid at all). Ask whether each is included
in the price or scoped separately. The full picture is on the
[revenue cycle management](/services/revenue-cycle-management) overview.

## What should you look for in a medical billing company?

Compare vendors on how they work, not only on what they promise. Five questions separate strong
partners from weak ones:

1. **Do they work inside your systems?** Some partners work in your practice-management system
   under your accounts. Others move your data into their own platform. Both can work, but the
   difference affects control, transparency, and what happens if you leave.
2. **Who is the billing provider of record?** In most arrangements your practice stays the billing
   provider and the partner provides billing labor. Confirm this in writing.
3. **Can you see everything?** You should be able to view claim status, denials, and A/R in your
   own system at any time, without asking for a report.
4. **Who actually does the work?** Ask whether you get a named person or a rotating pool, and how
   coverage works when that person is out.
5. **How are they paid?** The pricing model shapes behavior. See the table below.

### Common pricing models

| Model | How it works | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Flat monthly rate | Fixed fee based on agreed scope | Predictable cost; scope must be defined clearly |
| Percentage of collections | Fee is a share of what is collected | Aligns pay with collections; cost grows as revenue grows |
| Per-claim fee | Fee for each claim processed | Simple to model; can discourage working hard denials |

None of these is universally best. Percentage models can be attractive for a practice that wants
the vendor's pay tied directly to results. Flat-rate models are easier to budget and do not change
as your collections grow. Whichever you choose, ask what happens to the fee when volume spikes or
drops, and whether denial rework is included.

## What are the HIPAA and security considerations?

A billing partner will handle protected health information (PHI), so the legal and technical
basics are not optional. Require all of the following before anyone touches your data:

- **A signed Business Associate Agreement (BAA)** before any access is granted
- **Role-based access** limited to what the billing work needs, with unique logins per person
  (no shared credentials)
- **Multi-factor authentication** on any system that holds PHI
- **Access logging** so you can see who viewed or changed what
- **A clear offboarding process** that removes access the day someone leaves the account

If you want a structured way to evaluate any vendor that touches PHI, the

through the questions in detail, and the [HIPAA compliance page](/hipaa-compliance) explains how
Zenith approaches its own obligations as a Business Associate.

## What about losing control of your revenue cycle?

This is the most common concern, and it is fair. Outsourcing billing does not mean handing over
oversight. Protect yourself with a few simple controls:

- Keep your practice as the **billing provider** and keep payer accounts in your name
- Keep **read access** to claims, denials, and A/R in your own system
- Agree on a **reporting rhythm** (for example, a short monthly summary)
- Define **escalation rules**: which denials or balances need your sign-off
- Put **exit terms** in writing, including how your data and access are returned

Control comes from structure, not from doing the work yourself.

## How do you evaluate the ROI of outsourced billing?

Skip vendor averages and measure your own baseline. Before switching, record the following for at
least a few months:

- **Days in A/R:** how long, on average, it takes to collect after a claim is billed
- **Clean claim rate (first-pass acceptance):** the share of claims accepted without rework
- **Denial rate:** the share of claims denied on first submission
- **Net collection rate:** what you collected as a share of what you were contractually owed
- **Staff hours on revenue cycle tasks:** the time your team currently spends

Then compare total cost on both sides. For the in-house option, add salary, payroll taxes, benefits,
software seats, training, and the cost of coverage and turnover. For the outsourced option, add the
vendor fee plus the internal time you still spend on oversight. The right answer depends on your
volume, specialty, and payer mix, which is why a baseline beats any generic benchmark.

## What questions should you ask before signing a contract?

Bring this list to every vendor conversation:

1. What exactly is in scope, and what costs extra?
2. Will your team work in our practice-management system, or move our data elsewhere?
3. Who is the named point of contact, and who covers when they are out?
4. How are denials handled, and is rework included in the price?
5. What do we see each month, and can we view live status ourselves?
6. When is the BAA signed, and what access is granted on day one?
7. Where are your staff located, and how do they access PHI?
8. What is the minimum commitment, and how do we exit?
9. What happens to our data and access if we leave?

Clear, specific answers matter more than polished ones. Vague answers on scope or exit terms are a
warning sign.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does the billing vendor bill Medicare for us?

Not necessarily. In many arrangements your practice remains the billing provider and the partner
prepares and submits claims from your system under your accounts. Confirm the arrangement in
writing.

### How long does it take to switch billing partners?

It depends on scope, your systems, and how clean your current data is. Plan for a transition period
in which claims already in process are worked to completion while new claims move to the new team.
Ask any vendor to describe the timeline and who handles open A/R during the handoff.

### Is outsourced billing only for small practices?

No. Small practices often use it to avoid hiring a full team, while larger groups use it to add
capacity or cover a specific gap such as denial management or A/R follow-up.

### Should coding and credentialing be part of the same engagement?

They can be. Billing quality depends on coding accuracy and active payer enrollment, so many
practices review all three together. See [medical coding](/services/medical-coding) and
[provider credentialing](/services/provider-credentialing) for how each works.
