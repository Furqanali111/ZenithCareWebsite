A denied claim is revenue that has been earned but not paid. Each one costs staff time to review,
correct, and resubmit, and if nobody works it before the deadline, the money is gone. Most denials
are not mysteries. They come from a short list of causes that can be prevented at the front desk,
in coding, and in the claim-scrubbing step.

This guide explains the difference between a rejection and a denial, the most common causes, a
prevention workflow, and how to work and track the denials that still get through.

## What is the difference between a rejection and a denial?

People use the two words interchangeably, but they are different and are fixed in different ways.

| Term | What happened | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Rejection | The claim never entered the payer's system, usually because of a format or data error caught by the clearinghouse | Fix the error and resend. Nothing was decided yet. |
| Soft denial | The payer processed the claim and denied it for a fixable reason, such as missing information | Correct the problem and resubmit or reply with the missing item |
| Hard denial | The payer denied the claim as final, such as for a non-covered service | Appeal if you have grounds, or write off according to policy |

Rejections show up on clearinghouse reports within a day or two, so they need daily attention.
Denials arrive later on the remittance advice, so they need a tracking process.

## What are the most common causes of claim denials?

The same causes appear across most practices:

| Cause | What it looks like | How to prevent it |
|---|---|---|
| Eligibility or coverage issues | Coverage inactive, wrong plan, or coverage ended | Verify eligibility at scheduling and again at the visit |
| Missing or invalid prior authorization | Service required an approval that was not obtained | Track authorizations by service and payer before the visit |
| Coding errors | Diagnosis does not support the procedure, or a modifier is missing | Review coding before submission and track payer edits |
| Incomplete or wrong patient data | Misspelled name, wrong date of birth, wrong subscriber ID | Check demographics and insurance cards at every visit |
| Timely filing missed | Claim reached the payer after the filing deadline | Set internal deadlines well before the payer's limit |
| Duplicate claims | The same service submitted more than once | Track claim status before resubmitting |
| Medical necessity | Payer decided the service was not necessary for the diagnosis | Document clearly and match services to payer policy |
| Provider not enrolled | Provider not credentialed or enrolled with that payer | Confirm [enrollment](/services/provider-credentialing) before scheduling |
| Coordination of benefits | The wrong payer was billed first | Collect and verify all active coverage |

Notice how many of these start before the claim exists. A front-desk habit prevents more denials
than any amount of rework.

## How do you read a denial?

The remittance advice (the ERA or EOB) explains the payer's decision using standardized codes. Claim
adjustment reason codes (CARCs) say why a payment was adjusted, and remittance advice remark codes
(RARCs) add detail. Do not just record "denied." Record the code, because that is what lets you
spot patterns by payer and by cause.

## How do you prevent denials before they happen?

Build prevention into three stages.

### At the front end

- Verify eligibility and benefits at scheduling and at check-in
- Collect and confirm demographics and a copy of the current insurance card
- Check whether the planned service needs prior authorization, and get it before the visit
- Confirm the provider is enrolled with the patient's plan

### In the middle

- Make sure every service is captured as a charge
- Review coding against documentation, especially for new providers and new service lines
- Use [medical coding](/services/medical-coding) review to catch missing modifiers and mismatches

### At the back end

- Scrub claims for errors before sending them
- Work clearinghouse rejections every day
- Submit promptly, and track timely filing limits by payer

## How should you work a denial?

Set a repeatable routine, so nothing ages out:

1. **Log it.** Record the payer, the claim, the amount, the denial code, and the deadline.
2. **Triage.** Work the highest-dollar and closest-to-deadline items first.
3. **Decide.** Can you correct and resubmit, or is this an appeal?
4. **Act fast.** Correct and resubmit soft denials. For appeals, gather the medical record and the
   payer's policy and state your case clearly.
5. **Follow up.** Check the status on a set schedule until the claim is resolved.
6. **Learn.** Feed the cause back to whoever can prevent it next time.

### Know the deadlines

Appeal and filing deadlines are short and vary by payer. For example, Medicare has a filing limit
and a window to request a first-level appeal after the initial decision. Check the current rules for
each of your payers, write the deadlines into your tracking system, and treat them as hard stops.

## Which metrics tell you if it is working?

Track a few numbers over time and compare them to your own baseline, not to a generic benchmark:

- **Denial rate:** the share of claims denied on first submission
- **First-pass resolution rate:** the share paid without any rework
- **Days in A/R:** how long claims wait before payment
- **Appeal success rate:** how often appeals are overturned
- **Top denial reasons by payer:** where to focus prevention

If one payer or one reason keeps showing up, fix it at the source rather than reworking the same
claims every month.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many denials are normal?

There is no single right number, because it depends on specialty, payer mix, and claim types. The
useful comparison is your own rate over time. A rising denial rate or a growing pile of unworked
denials is the signal to act.

### Should we appeal every denial?

No. Appeal when you have a solid basis, the amount justifies the effort, and the deadline allows
it. Many denials are soft and are fixed faster by correcting the claim and resubmitting.

### Who should own denials in the practice?

One named person should own the process, with a clear way to hand problems to coding, the front
desk, or providers. Denials that belong to everyone tend to be worked by no one.

### Can better documentation reduce denials?

Yes. Many medical necessity and coding denials trace back to documentation that does not clearly
support the service billed. Feedback from denial patterns is one of the best inputs for provider
documentation training.
