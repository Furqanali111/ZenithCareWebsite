Obtaining and documenting patient consent is the first billing gate in
[Chronic Care Management](/services/chronic-care-management). A practice can have perfectly logged
clinical staff time and a complete care plan, and still face a Medicare audit recoupment if consent
was not obtained and documented correctly before billing began.

This guide covers exactly what CMS requires, what must appear in the patient record, the most common
documentation mistakes, and how annual re-consent and revocation work.

## Why does CCM consent matter?

Medicare requires consent for CCM because the program changes two things for the patient:

1. **Cost-sharing:** CCM services are subject to the standard Medicare Part B coinsurance, typically
   20 percent. Patients should know they may owe a monthly cost-sharing amount before they are
   enrolled.
2. **Exclusivity:** only one billing provider can bill CCM for the same patient in the same calendar
   month. If the patient sees multiple practices, they should know that enrolling with one prevents
   another from billing in the same period.

CMS included consent as a hard requirement precisely to prevent patients from being enrolled without
their knowledge and then receiving an unexpected bill. When consent is missing or poorly documented,
Medicare can treat every claim in the affected period as an improper payment and recover it.

## What does CMS require for verbal and written consent?

CMS allows either verbal or written consent. Either form is legally sufficient. Both must be
documented in the patient's medical record.

**Required elements of the consent conversation:**

1. A description of CCM: what care coordination services will be provided, the general nature of
   the work (calls, care plan reviews, medication management, referral coordination)
2. The cost-sharing obligation: that the patient will owe their standard Part B coinsurance for
   CCM each month (approximately 20 percent of the allowed amount, unless supplemental coverage
   applies)
3. The exclusivity rule: that only one provider may bill CCM for the patient in any calendar month,
   so enrolling with this practice means another provider cannot separately bill CCM for the same
   period
4. The right to revoke: that the patient may withdraw consent at any time without affecting their
   access to other care from the practice

**Verbal consent:** the clinical staff member conducts the consent conversation, documents the date,
their name, and that each of the four elements above was covered. A specific EHR note or structured
consent template is strongly preferred over a generic "patient consented to CCM" entry.

**Written consent:** the patient signs a consent form that includes the four required elements. A
copy is filed in the medical record or uploaded to the EHR. Written consent is easier to produce
during an audit and is the recommended approach for new CCM programs.

## What must be documented in the patient record?

For audit readiness, each enrolled patient's record should contain:

| Document | What it should show |
|---|---|
| Consent record | Date, staff member who obtained consent, elements covered (verbal) OR signed form (written) |
| Initial care plan | Patient's chronic conditions, goals, medications, care team, emergency plan |
| Monthly time log | Date of each CCM activity, activity type, duration, staff member |
| Communication records | Notes on calls, messages, referrals, and care coordination contacts |
| Care plan updates | Date and nature of any updates made during the month |

The consent record is separate from the care plan and time log. Auditors look for all three types
of documentation independently. A complete time log with no consent record is not a safe audit
posture.

CMS does not require a specific consent form template. The documentation simply needs to demonstrate
that consent was obtained and the required elements were covered. However, using a consistent
template across all enrolled patients makes audit responses faster and more uniform.

## What are the most common consent documentation mistakes?

In order of risk level:

**1. Billing before consent is documented.** This is the highest-risk mistake. The claim date
must follow the consent date. If the EHR shows a CCM claim dated before any consent documentation,
Medicare will treat that claim, and potentially all subsequent claims for the same patient, as
improper.

**2. Missing one or more required elements.** A note that says "patient agreed to CCM enrollment"
without addressing cost-sharing or the exclusivity rule is not sufficient. Each of the four required
elements should be visible in the documentation.

**3. No documentation at all.** Some practices obtain verbal consent over the phone without any
EHR entry. This is legally sufficient only if the conversation is later documented. An undocumented
verbal consent has the same audit risk as no consent.

**4. Consent by non-clinical staff.** The person who conducts the consent conversation should be
qualified clinical staff or a provider. Consent obtained by a front-desk or billing staff member
is not appropriate for a clinical enrollment.

**5. No re-consent after a significant program change.** If the practice changes its CCM vendor or
substantially changes the services delivered, re-consenting existing patients is good practice
even if not strictly required by CMS for every program change.

## Do patients need to re-consent annually?

CMS does not currently require annual re-consent for CCM. Once a patient has provided valid consent
and is actively enrolled, the consent carries forward into subsequent months and years.

Best practice is still to revisit the enrollment discussion annually, typically at the patient's
annual wellness visit. This serves two purposes:

1. It reconfirms the patient's intent to remain enrolled and their awareness of the cost-sharing
   obligation, which may have changed.
2. It creates a documented touchpoint that strengthens the audit record.

Some practices build a brief "CCM check-in" into the annual wellness visit note: "Patient confirmed
awareness of CCM enrollment, cost-sharing structure, and right to revoke at any time."

## How does revocation work?

A patient may revoke CCM consent at any time by notifying the practice verbally or in writing. The
practice must:

1. Stop billing CCM for that patient immediately upon receiving the revocation.
2. Document the date and method of revocation in the patient record.
3. Not bill for any CCM services delivered after the revocation date.

Note on partial-month revocations: if a patient revokes mid-month after 20 or more minutes of CCM
work have already been delivered and documented, the practice may bill for that month. If revocation
comes before 20 minutes are documented, no claim should be submitted.

The patient may re-enroll in CCM in a subsequent month if they change their mind. Re-enrollment
requires a new consent.

## How does an outsourced CCM partner handle consent?

A CCM vendor handles consent differently depending on the practice's workflow and the vendor's model.
The two most common approaches:

**Practice-led consent:** the practice's clinical staff obtains consent at a scheduled office visit
or annual wellness visit, and the vendor receives the signed or documented consent before
beginning monthly coordination. This keeps the consent conversation in the primary patient
relationship and is generally preferred by practices that want to maintain high enrollment rates.

**Vendor-assisted consent:** the vendor sends a consent letter or makes an initial enrollment call,
explains the program, documents consent, and routes the confirmation back to the practice's EHR.
This approach can work well for practices with a large potential panel and limited staff time for
consent conversations.

Either way, the vendor must operate under a Business Associate Agreement (BAA) with the practice,
and all consent documentation must be accessible in the practice's EHR or medical record system.
